Liquidity Planning

What Happens Financially After Selling a Business

The 90 days after a liquidity event are the most consequential — and the most likely to be mishandled.

The 90 days after a liquidity event are the most consequential — and the most likely to be mishandled.

For years, the biggest financial risk in an owner's life was concentrated in one place: the business. The day it sells, that risk doesn't disappear. It changes shape. A single illiquid asset becomes a single liquid number, and the decisions that number requires are unlike anything the sale itself demanded.

The gap nobody prepares for

Selling a business is usually the most-advised moment of an owner's financial life — bankers, attorneys, deal advisors, all focused intensely on getting the transaction closed. The day after it closes, most of that attention disappears. The deal team's job is done. What's left is a number in an account and a set of decisions nobody was specifically preparing the owner to make.

This gap matters because the instincts that built a successful business are not the instincts a sudden lump sum rewards. An owner spent years reinvesting aggressively, tolerating illiquidity, and betting on one asset. Proceeds sitting in cash reward almost the opposite: patience, diversification, and a plan built around when the money is actually needed rather than what feels productive to do with it immediately.

The three mistakes that show up first

Treating it as spendable before it's sorted. Sale proceeds arrive as one number, but they are never really one pile of money — some of it is tax liability that hasn't been paid yet, some is meant to replace years of future income, some is genuinely available to deploy. Spending or investing against the gross number, before it's been separated into what it actually is, is the single most common first mistake.

Moving too fast in either direction. Some owners feel pressure to "put the money to work" immediately, replicating the urgency that served them in the business. Others do the opposite — leaving the full amount in cash indefinitely because nothing feels as safe as the business did. Both extremes usually get corrected later, at a cost.

Underestimating the tax timeline. Depending on deal structure, taxes on the sale may be owed well before the full picture of the transaction is finalized — installment payments, earnouts, or escrow releases can all change what's actually owed and when. Assuming the number in the account is the number available to use is a common and expensive miscalculation.

Re-sorting the proceeds by time horizon

The same three-tier logic that applies to any capital works here, just applied all at once instead of gradually: what's needed soon, what needs to be protected for a defined stretch, and what has a genuinely long horizon.

QuestionWhat it determines
What has to be set aside for taxes not yet paid?This isn't proceeds at all — it's already spoken for
What replaces income for the next 1–3 years while a new plan takes shape?Operating and Safety Capital — needs to be stable and available, not at risk
What genuinely won't be touched for years?Growth Capital — the only portion where taking on real risk is appropriate

Most of the early mistakes above come from skipping this sorting step entirely and treating the full number as if it belonged in one category.

The identity shift underneath the financial one

There's a psychological dimension that's easy to underweight. For years, net worth was tied to something an owner built and controlled directly. After a sale, it becomes a number managed through other people's decisions and market conditions the owner has no influence over. That shift is disorienting even when the sale is a clear financial win, and it's part of why the first 90 days are so often mishandled — the decisions are being made by someone still adjusting to a completely different relationship with their own wealth.

Acknowledging that adjustment period as real, rather than something to push through quickly, tends to produce better decisions than pretending the transition is purely financial.

The first 90 days, done well

The owners who navigate this cleanly tend to do a version of the same thing: pause before deploying anything, get the true after-tax number confirmed rather than assumed, sort what's left by time horizon before touching any of it, and treat the first few months as a planning period rather than a decision-making sprint. The sale was the hard part for years. What happens next deserves the same level of deliberateness, even though the transaction itself is already done.
