Building the Right Advisory Team for Capital Decisions

Most owners hire vendors. The owners who keep what they build assemble coordinated teams. Here's the difference.

The broker doesn't know what the CPA is planning. The CPA doesn't know what the attorney drafted last year. The attorney has never spoken to whoever manages the investment accounts.

Most business owners have all four. Few of them have ever been in a room together.

Vendors versus a team

There's a real difference between hiring a set of vendors and building a coordinated team, and most owners don't discover which one they have until a decision actually needs all four perspectives at once — a sale, a large distribution, an estate plan, a buy-sell funding gap — and it becomes clear that nobody has been talking to anybody.

A vendor relationship looks like this: you call your CPA in March for taxes. You call your attorney when a document needs drafting. You call whoever manages your investments when the statement looks off. Each one does competent, specific work. None of them is thinking about what the others are doing, because that was never part of the arrangement.

A coordinated team looks different in one specific way: at least one person in the group is responsible for knowing what the others are doing, and flagging it when something one advisor is planning creates a problem — or an opportunity — for what another is working on.

Where the gaps actually cost money

The expensive mistakes rarely come from any one advisor doing bad work. They come from the seams between good work.

A few patterns show up often enough to be worth naming directly:

  • The tax strategy nobody structured around. A CPA identifies a real opportunity — an entity restructuring, a timing shift, a deduction strategy — but it requires a document change only the attorney can make, and the attorney was never looped in before the filing deadline passed.
  • The estate plan built on stale numbers. An attorney drafts a plan based on a net worth and asset mix from two years ago, because nobody flagged that the business has grown, a property was sold, or a large distribution changed the picture materially.
  • The buy-sell agreement with no funding behind it. A legal document exists. Nobody coordinated with an advisor on what actually pays for it if it's triggered tomorrow. The agreement is real; the money behind it is not.
  • The investment strategy that ignores the exit. Money keeps getting managed the same way for years, without anyone connecting it to a liquidity event that's now two or three years out and should be changing how that capital is positioned.

None of these are failures of expertise. They're failures of one advisor not knowing what another one already knows.

What coordination actually requires

It doesn't require replacing your CPA, your attorney, or anyone else you already trust. It requires someone whose job includes asking a different question than "is my piece of this done correctly" — namely, "does my piece of this still make sense given what everyone else is doing."

In practice, that looks like:

  • A shared, current picture of the full balance sheet — business and personal — that more than one advisor can actually see, not one that lives only in the owner's head.
  • A standing check, at least annually, on whether a change in one area (a tax strategy, a legal document, an investment shift) has downstream effects on the others.
  • One person who treats "coordination" as an actual responsibility, not an assumption that it's happening because everyone is competent individually.

The test worth applying

A simple way to check which one you actually have: ask your CPA what your attorney's current estate plan assumes about your business's value. If the honest answer is "I don't know," that's not a reflection on either professional. It's a reflection of the fact that nobody has been assigned to know.

Most owners hire vendors. The owners who keep more of what they build are the ones who eventually notice the gap and assemble a team instead.
