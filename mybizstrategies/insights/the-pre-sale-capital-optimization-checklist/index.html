The Pre-Sale Capital Optimization Checklist

Most of what determines whether a sale goes well financially happens before a buyer ever appears.

Most of what determines whether a sale goes well financially happens before a buyer ever appears. By the time there's a term sheet on the table, most of the structural decisions that would have improved the outcome are already locked in — for better or worse.

This isn't a checklist to run through during due diligence. It's a checklist worth running through now, whether a sale is two years out or not yet on the calendar at all — because everything on it takes time to fix, and none of it can be fixed retroactively once a deal is in motion.

Before There's a Buyer

Know the real number, not the hoped-for one. Most owners have a figure in mind for what the business is worth. Few have had that figure tested against how buyers or the market would actually value it today, using current multiples and current financials — not the number from three years ago, and not the number that would feel satisfying. Getting an honest valuation, even an informal one, exposes the actual gap between where the business is and where an owner assumes it is.

Separate personal and business finances completely. Any commingling — a vehicle, a property, a family member on payroll for reasons unrelated to the role — becomes a diligence question during a sale and a valuation discount before one. Cleaning this up takes time to unwind properly; it can't be done the month before a deal closes.

Confirm what actually happens to existing debt and guarantees at a sale. Personal guarantees on business debt don't necessarily disappear at closing — depending on structure, they may need to be explicitly released as part of the transaction. Knowing this in advance, rather than discovering it during negotiation, changes how a deal gets structured from the start.

While Building Toward a Sale

Reduce customer and revenue concentration where possible. A business where one customer represents a large share of revenue is priced differently — and often perceived as riskier — than one with a diversified base. This is one of the few items on this list that takes real operational time to address, which is exactly why it needs to start years, not months, before a sale.

Document the processes that currently live only in your head. A business that depends entirely on the owner's daily involvement is worth less to a buyer than one with systems and a team that can run without them. This shows up directly in valuation and directly in how smooth a transition period looks to a buyer evaluating risk.

Understand the tax structure question before a deal exists. As covered in Tax-Efficient Exit Structures, many of the most valuable tax strategies require lead time measured in years, not months. This is a conversation worth having with a tax advisor well before a sale is a certainty.

In the Twelve Months Before a Likely Sale

Get real professional guidance assembled, not just retained. A deal attorney, a tax advisor familiar with exit structures, and someone thinking about where proceeds will actually go afterward should ideally be in conversation with each other before a deal is signed — not introduced to one another during closing week.

Model what the after-tax, after-debt number actually looks like. The headline sale price and the number that actually lands in an account are often very different figures once debt payoff, transaction costs, and taxes are accounted for. Modeling this in advance prevents the number from being a surprise at the worst possible moment.

Decide, at least roughly, what the proceeds are for. Not every dollar needs a plan today, but having a rough sense of what portion replaces income, what portion is truly long-term, and what obligations need to be covered first makes the post-sale sorting process dramatically less chaotic when the money actually arrives.

The Pattern Across All Of It

Nearly everything on this list has the same property: it's inexpensive and manageable when there's no deal pressure, and difficult or impossible to fix once a buyer is at the table. The owners who get the best outcomes aren't the ones who found a better negotiator. They're the ones who started preparing before preparation felt urgent.
